\section{Introduction}

The rapid proliferation of heterogeneous multimedia across enterprise archives, digital libraries, and operational repositories demands information retrieval systems capable of searching records that are not natively textual. In enterprise document workflows—such as healthcare systems digitizing millions of paper patient intake records, legal firms archiving scanned court proceedings, and supply chain enterprises indexing procurement invoices—organizations process massive volumes of unstructured physical records daily. Transforming these documents into queryable digital assets is essential for business intelligence, compliance auditing, and decision support \cite{jing2024llmvectordb}. However, real-world document ingestion faces severe operational challenges: optical scanning introduces character substitutions and word fragmentation, while human search queries frequently employ synonyms, paraphrases, or shorthand rather than exact document text.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) \cite{lewis2020rag} addresses the hallucination tendencies of Large Language Models (LLMs) by grounding responses in retrieved domain documents. Recently, multimodal extensions of RAG have emerged to handle non-textual data, as comprehensively surveyed by Abootorabi et al. \cite{abootorabi2025multimodal}. For example, Patel et al. \cite{patel2025multimodal} presented an enterprise multimodal RAG pipeline employing an OCR document ingestion module, ImageBind embeddings indexed in a Milvus vector database, and fine-tuned LLaMA-3.

In a proxy-mediated design, three practical failure modes remain important:

\begin{enumerate}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{1pt plus 0.5pt}
    \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{Single-Stage Dense Retrieval Pitfalls:} Relying exclusively on single-stage dense vector similarity per turn can induce semantic drift. Dense embeddings may overlook exact lexical tokens, numerical identifiers, item codes, and proper nouns that sparse inverted indexes can match directly \cite{robertson2009bm25}.
    \item \textbf{Absence of Candidate Score Recalibration:} A bi-encoder score alone does not expose token-level agreement with a candidate's proxy text. A lightweight second-stage scorer can therefore be useful before context assembly, even when it is not a neural cross-encoder.
    \item \textbf{Failure on Multi-Hop Queries Across Modalities:} A single retrieval pass does not ensure that a fact recovered from one modality becomes a useful probe for another. Iterative bridging can improve lexical reachability, but it also introduces cascading extraction errors that must be measured rather than assumed away.
\end{enumerate}

To investigate these trade-offs, this paper presents a text-proxy-grounded multi-stage, multi-hop Multimodal RAG architecture and evaluates the empirical validity of adaptive fusion, query routing, and reliability weighting under a pre-specified experimental protocol across held-out test splits\footnote{All benchmark collections, evaluation harnesses, and reproduction scripts are publicly available at: \url{https://github.com/koushik2109/MemoryVerse}}.

\textbf{Key Contributions:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{1pt plus 0.5pt}
    \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{Sub-Word Inverted Index Dominance and Scope Conditions on Optical OCR:} We refute the assumption that word-level BM25 is superior under optical OCR. Across both proprietary Apple Vision and open-source Tesseract 5.5 LSTM OCR ($N=306$ probes, 813 corpus items), character 3--5gram BM25 is the single largest sparse architectural lift, achieving decisive retrieval superiority under verbatim optical noise (+0.1065 clean, +0.1276 degraded blur, +0.1049 clean Tesseract; all $p_{\text{Holm}} < 10^{-4}$). We delineate its strict scope boundary: this dominance applies to verbatim receipt searches where queries share exact character substrings with documents; on multi-field administrative forms the verbatim margin narrows to a statistical tie (+0.0069, 0.8433 vs.\ 0.8364), and under entity-substituting light paraphrases, word BM25 drops to 0.1573 while character BM25 achieves 0.2228 and dense BGE achieves 0.1834. On receipts, document-native visual retrieval (ColQwen2-v1.0) achieves 0.4985 MRR under light paraphrases (+0.0982 lift over Word BM25, $p = 4 \times 10^{-5}$), but statistically ties BM25 on answerable questions (0.9257 vs.\ 0.9450, $p = 0.109$) while incurring 286$\times$ query latency (249.21\,ms vs.\ 0.87\,ms).
    \item \textbf{Dense Retrieval Collapse Mechanism and Query Formulation Controls:} We demonstrate that dense bi-encoder collapse on document text is primarily driven by single-entity query-span disconnect rather than sequence truncation constraints (real $\text{max\_seq\_length}=256$; 33.4\% exceed 256 tokens, but chunked MaxP yields identical MRR 0.2030 vs.\ 0.2056). On pristine ground truth text, isolated verbatim lookups yield MRR 0.1919 (MiniLM) and 0.3256 (BGE-Base); co-specifying multiple anchor entities or formulating queries as natural answerable questions improves BGE-Base MRR to 0.5500 (keyword control, Hit@10 78.4\%) and 0.5263 (questions, Hit@10 79.7\%) without modifying document representations.
    \item \textbf{Dose-Response Boundary of Query Mismatch vs.\ Optical Degradation:} We establish that query mismatch dominates optical noise under modern neural OCR (Apple Vision light paraphrase drops 0.4321 MRR vs.\ blur drop of 0.1600). However, under open-source Tesseract 5.5, severe optical blur collapses Word BM25 by 0.3916 MRR (0.7364 to 0.3448), which is comparable in magnitude to the semantic paraphrase drop on clean text (0.3400 MRR). For answerable factual questions, topical anchors preserve high discriminability (BM25 MRR 0.8937--0.9192).
    \item \textbf{Finite-Sample Marginal Conformal Cascades:} Grounded in the distribution-free risk-controlling prediction framework of Bates et al.~\cite{bates2021conformal} and Angelopoulos et al.~\cite{angelopoulos2021gentle}, we evaluate conformal cascade calibration under marginal loss $L = \mathbf{1}[\text{not escalated} \land Y \ne 1]$ across 200 cluster-stratified splits ($N=2{,}754$ instances). With finite-sample $(n\hat{R}+1)/(n+1) \le \alpha$ adjustment, mean test miss risk is rigorously controlled at 0.0494 ($\le 0.05$) at an escalation rate of 78.4\% (21.6\% computation savings vs.\ always-escalating cross-encoders).
    \item \textbf{Independent Multi-Hop Bridging Verification:} Across separate, non-copied pipelines on 50 diagnostic multi-hop queries, we demonstrate that rule-based extractive pseudo-relevance feedback exhibits no detectable difference from generative LLM bridging (0.640 vs.\ 0.640 Target Hit@10, paired diff $0.0000$ [95\% CI: $-0.0792, +0.0792$], exact McNemar $p = 1.0000$); because the 95\% confidence interval spans beyond $\pm 0.05$, non-inferiority is not formally established, though extractive bridging matches generative accuracy without LLM inference latency or financial cost (expanded 100-case suite with 50 single-hop controls yields 0.820 vs.\ 0.820).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Multimodal Representation and Retrieval}
Early cross-modal retrieval systems relied on dual-encoder contrastive learning. Models like CLIP \cite{radford2021clip} and SigLIP \cite{zhai2023siglip} project images and text into a shared latent space using sigmoid or softmax cross-entropy objectives. ImageBind \cite{girdhar2023imagebind} extended this paradigm across six modalities (images, text, video, audio, depth, and thermal) using images as the central binding modality. While effective for broad semantic matching, dual-encoders compress multimedia into fixed-dimension vectors, frequently losing granular lexical and structural tokens. Our hybrid design combines dense embeddings with sparse lexical representations such as BM25 \cite{robertson2009bm25} to retain both semantic intent and exact token matches. Unlike single-modality dual-encoders that discard fine-grained lexical identifiers, our framework analyzes the complementary regime boundaries where character-level sparse indexing outperforms dense representations under optical noise.

\subsection{Multimodal RAG and Document Analysis}
Integrating multimodal inputs into RAG workflows has gained rapid momentum with the emergence of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and advanced LLMs. Recent native multimodal document retrievers such as ColPali \cite{faysse2024colpali} and M3DocRAG \cite{cho2024m3docrag}, benchmarked on the Visual Document Retrieval (ViDoRe) suite \cite{faysse2024vidore}, index document pages using multi-vector vision-language representations to bypass optical character recognition. While promising, multi-vector VLM representations require substantial GPU memory and heavy cross-attention compute during query serving. Conversely, enterprise systems such as Patel et al. \cite{patel2025multimodal} and Joshi et al. \cite{joshi2024multimodel} mediate retrieval through grounded text proxies, combining OCR document ingestion, multimodal embeddings, and LLM generation. Our work investigates this text-proxy paradigm: we evaluate the empirical trade-offs of hybrid dense--sparse fusion, candidate scoring, and iterative query decomposition, systematically delineating where text proxies excel and where native visual representations remain necessary. Unlike ColPali \cite{faysse2024colpali} which mandates multi-vector VLM computation for every candidate page regardless of query complexity, our regime analysis establishes the exact conditions under which sub-millisecond CPU text proxies match or exceed native vision models, identifying when GPU-heavy inference is truly justified.

\subsection{Multi-Hop Reasoning in Retrieval Systems}
Multi-hop retrieval addresses queries where no single document contains the complete answer \cite{xiong2021multihop, khattab2021baleen}. While multi-hop text RAG systems (such as Baleen \cite{khattab2021baleen} and Self-Ask \cite{press2023selfask}) have established iterative query reformulation methods, multi-hop retrieval across \textit{heterogeneous multimodal collections} remains less systematically evaluated. Our diagnostic targets the case in which the second-hop keyword is absent from the original query and must be recovered from first-hop proxy context; it does not claim that all multimodal QA benchmarks assume co-located evidence. Unlike prior multi-hop pipelines that rely on expensive generative LLMs for every hop, we empirically show that rule-based extractive pseudo-relevance feedback achieves parity in retrieval reachability with zero generative token latency or API cost.

\subsection{Adaptive Fusion, Cascade Ranking, and Selective Reranking}
In information retrieval, static fusion coefficients fail to account for query-level variance. Query Performance Prediction (QPP) \cite{carmel2010qpp} and adaptive fusion techniques dynamically adjust interpolation weights between sparse and dense scores based on query ambiguity or lexical specificity. Similarly, cascade ranking architectures \cite{wang2011cascade, gallagher2019cascade} balance retrieval efficiency and ranking precision by passing top candidates through successively more expressive models. In monomodal web search, cascades evaluate candidates using fixed score thresholds or supervised early-exit classifiers. However, existing cascade rankers do not account for multimodal proxy corruption (e.g., OCR or ASR transcription noise) or cross-modal query routing. As summarized in Table~\ref{tab:paradigm_comparison}, we formulate and empirically evaluate candidate mechanisms—including surface-feature query routing, confidence-margin cascades ($\Delta_{1,2} < \tau_{\text{margin}}$), and observable proxy reliability discounting ($r_m(d)$)—to determine whether lightweight heuristic defenses provide measurable utility over standard inverted indexes. Unlike prior cascade ranking that relies on uncalibrated heuristic margins, we ground cascade escalation in distribution-free conformal risk control to guarantee test-set error bounds.

\subsection{Optical Character Recognition Quality and Information Retrieval}
Optical Character Recognition (OCR) systems translate physical document imagery into digital character sequences, but practical scanning routinely introduces character substitutions, insertions, deletions, and bounding-box permutations \cite{smith2007tesseract, fateh2024ocr}. Foundational information retrieval studies have established that standard word-level tokenizers (such as inverted indexes using whitespace and punctuation delimiters) suffer catastrophic vocabulary mismatch when typographical errors fracture keyword strings. Early mitigation strategies, including Otsu thresholding \cite{otsu1979} and Hough transforms \cite{hough1962} for skew correction, focused on image-domain preprocessing to boost OCR accuracy. However, in downstream information retrieval, post-OCR lexical degradation leads to subword tokenization fragmentation in subword-based language models and word omissions in lexical indexes. Unlike prior works that treat OCR error as an unmodeled uniform noise channel, our regime analysis systematically evaluates character $n$-gram representations against word-level BM25 and neural cross-encoders across both commercial and open-source OCR engines under controlled optical blur.

\subsection{Conformal Prediction and Risk-Controlled Cascades}
In mission-critical information retrieval, deploying neural cross-encoders or multi-vector Vision-Language Models for every candidate query imposes unsustainable serving latency and energy expenditure. Cascade ranking pipelines address this bottleneck by evaluating candidates through tiered stages, escalating to expressive neural models only when preliminary scores indicate ranking ambiguity \cite{wang2011cascade, gallagher2019cascade}. However, conventional cascade systems rely on empirical score margins or heuristic thresholds that offer no formal statistical guarantees on retrieval accuracy. Conformal prediction, formulated by Bates et~al.~\cite{bates2021conformal} and surveyed by Angelopoulos and Bates~\cite{angelopoulos2021gentle}, provides a distribution-free, finite-sample framework for bounding user-specified risk metrics without restrictive distributional assumptions. By framing the decision to escalate candidates as a risk-controlling prediction problem, conformal prediction enables retrieval systems to bound the probability of unescalated retrieval errors strictly below a pre-specified tolerance $\alpha$. In this work, we ground production cascade ranking in finite-sample marginal conformal risk control, establishing rigorous mathematical miss-rate guarantees while reducing computational costs.

The proposed Multi-Stage Multimodal RAG architecture is structured into four functional phases (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}): (1) Heterogeneous Multimodal Ingestion and Preprocessing, (2) Hybrid Dense-Sparse First-Stage Retrieval, (3) Dual-Signal Candidate Reranking, and (4) Multi-Hop Cross-Modal Reasoning.

\subsection{Heterogeneous Ingestion and Preprocessing Pipeline}
To process unstructured enterprise multimedia, our architecture mediates retrieval through grounded textual proxies. A deployment can obtain these proxies from OCR text \cite{smith2007tesseract}, image captions \cite{li2022blip}, speech transcripts \cite{radford2023whisper}, and acoustic metadata \cite{piczak2015esc50}. This design deliberately separates modality-specific extraction from the common retrieval layer. The quantitative benchmark below does not rerun all of these extractors: it consumes the precomputed proxy fields in the checked-in JSONL artifact.

\textbf{1. Document and Text Ingestion:} The reference document path decomposes scanned administrative forms into page images, optionally applies Otsu binarization \cite{otsu1979} and Hough-transform skew correction \cite{hough1962, fateh2024ocr}, and obtains OCR text $T_d$ with bounding boxes via Tesseract \cite{smith2007tesseract}.
\textbf{2. Images and Photographic Content:} A deployment may use a vision-language captioner such as BLIP \cite{li2022blip} to generate a descriptive representation $C_i$. In the reported artifact, image \texttt{text\_representation} fields are instead constructed by concatenating the English query annotations associated with each image; they are not newly generated BLIP captions.
\textbf{3. Audio Recordings:} A deployment can append time-aligned speech transcription from Whisper \cite{radford2023whisper} and acoustic event metadata. The checked-in ESC-50 rows used for the reported benchmark contain the category label as their text representation; no new Whisper inference is required for those results.

All processed items are stored in a dual layout: dense embeddings are generated via Sentence-BERT (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) \cite{reimers2019sbert} and BGE (\texttt{bge-base-en-v1.5}) \cite{xiao2023bge}, while lexical tokens are indexed with BM25 \cite{robertson2009bm25, robertson1995okapi}. Milvus \cite{wang2021milvus} and HNSW \cite{malkov2020hnsw} are compatible deployment choices, but the offline benchmark scripts use an in-memory BM25 index and a dense embedding matrix; no Milvus service is used for the reported measurements.

\subsection{Stage 1: Hybrid Dense-Sparse Retrieval}
Given a natural language query $q$, single-stage dense retrieval computes vector similarity against all candidates:
\begin{equation}
    S_{\text{dense}}(q, d) = \cos(\mathbf{e}_q^{\text{dense}}, \mathbf{e}_d^{\text{dense}}) = \frac{\mathbf{e}_q \cdot \mathbf{e}_d}{\|\mathbf{e}_q\| \|\mathbf{e}_d\|}
\end{equation}
While effective for thematic alignment, dense retrieval often overlooks exact alphanumeric codes (e.g., serial numbers, dates, person names). Conversely, sparse BM25 retrieval excels at lexical precision:
\begin{equation}
    S_{\text{sparse}}(q, d) = \sum_{t \in q} \text{IDF}(t) \cdot \frac{f(t, d) \cdot (k_1 + 1)}{f(t, d) + k_1 \cdot \left(1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|d|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)}
\end{equation}
Because dense cosine scores lie in $[-1, 1]$ while BM25 scores are unbounded $[0, \infty)$, combining raw scores directly is unstable. We evaluate two score harmonization strategies:

\textbf{Method A: Normalized Convex Combination:}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    S_{\text{hybrid}}(q, d) ={} & \alpha_{\text{dense}} \cdot \hat{S}_{\text{dense}}(q, d) \\
    & + (1 - \alpha_{\text{dense}}) \cdot \hat{S}_{\text{sparse}}(q, d)
\end{aligned}
    \label{eq:hybrid}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{S}_{\text{dense}}$ and $\hat{S}_{\text{sparse}}$ denote min-max normalized scores over the candidate pool, and $\alpha_{\text{dense}} \in [0, 1]$ parameterizes dense vs.\ sparse weighting ($\alpha_{\text{sparse}} = 1 - \alpha_{\text{dense}}$).

\textbf{Method B: Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF):}
To avoid batch-dependent min-max sensitivity, RRF \cite{cormack2009rrf} fuses ranked lists directly without score scale calibration (Eq.~\ref{eq:rrf}):
\begin{equation}
    \text{RRF}(q, d) = \frac{1}{k + r_{\text{dense}}(d)} + \frac{1}{k + r_{\text{sparse}}(d)}
    \label{eq:rrf}
\end{equation}
where $r_{\text{dense}}(d)$ and $r_{\text{sparse}}(d)$ are the ordinal ranks of candidate $d$, and $k$ is a smoothing constant ($k = 60$). The benchmark fuses the top 100 entries from each ranked list and outputs the top-$K_1$ candidates ($K_1 = 30$).

\subsection{Stage 2: Dual-Signal Candidate Reranking}
Bi-encoders compute query and document representations independently, preventing fine-grained token-level cross-interaction. While neural cross-encoders provide deep token interaction, scoring candidate pools with a full cross-encoder adds substantial latency overhead (as benchmarked in Table~\ref{tab:system_costs}). As an efficient alternative, Stage 2 deploys a lightweight dual-signal candidate reranker that scores each candidate $d$ in the top-$K_1$ pool using joint semantic similarity and exact token coverage:
\begin{equation}
    S_{\text{rerank}}(q, d) = \beta \cdot S_{\text{dense}}(q, d) + (1 - \beta) \cdot \text{Overlap}(q, d)
\end{equation}
where $\text{Overlap}(q, d) = \frac{|\text{Tokens}(q) \cap \text{Tokens}(d)|}{|\text{Tokens}(q)|}$ evaluates lowercase token coverage in candidate text representations, and $\beta = 0.65$ is a fixed configuration in the benchmark implementation. Because the supplied evaluation artifact has no development/test partition, this value is not claimed to be independently tuned. All $K_1 = 30$ candidates retrieved from Stage 1 are reranked. For generation context assembly, the top $K_2 = 5$ items are injected into the generator prompt, while top $K = 10$ items are evaluated in the diagnostic scripts.

\subsection{Stage 3: Multi-Hop Cross-Modal Reasoning}
To resolve compositional queries that span modalities, we introduce an iterative query decomposition and entity bridging algorithm.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Multi-Hop Cross-Modal Retrieval Procedure}
\label{alg:multihop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\small
\REQUIRE User Query $Q$, Max Hops $H_{\max}=2$, Candidate Index $\mathcal{D}$, Decomposer LLM $\mathcal{M}_{\text{decomp}}$
\ENSURE Grounded Multimodal Context $\mathcal{C}^*$
\STATE Decompose $Q$ via $\mathcal{M}_{\text{decomp}}$ into sub-query plan: $\mathcal{G} = \{q_1, q_2\}$
\STATE Initialize Context Set $\mathcal{C}^* \leftarrow \emptyset$, Accumulated Facts $\mathcal{F} \leftarrow \emptyset$
\FOR{$h = 1$ to $H_{\max}$}
    \STATE Formulate search probe $q_h' \leftarrow \text{Bridge}(q_h, \mathcal{F})$ via $\mathcal{M}_{\text{decomp}}$
    \STATE Retrieve top-$K_1$ candidates via Hybrid Fusion: $\mathcal{R}_1 \leftarrow \text{Retrieve}_{\text{hybrid}}(q_h', \mathcal{D})$
    \STATE Rerank candidate pool: $\mathcal{R}_2 \leftarrow \text{Rerank}(\mathcal{R}_1, q_h')$
    \STATE Extract bridging entities: $\mathcal{E}_h \leftarrow \text{ExtractEntities}(\mathcal{R}_2[:3])$
    \IF{$\text{Confidence}(\mathcal{E}_h) < \tau$ \textbf{or} $\mathcal{E}_h \in \mathcal{V}_{\text{refusal}}$}
        \STATE \COMMENT{Confidence gate: fallback to single-pass compound search to prevent drift}
        \STATE $\mathcal{C}^* \leftarrow \text{Retrieve}_{\text{hybrid}}(Q, \mathcal{D})[:5]$
        \STATE \textbf{break}
    \ENDIF
    \STATE Update $\mathcal{F} \leftarrow \mathcal{F} \cup \mathcal{E}_h$
    \STATE Update $\mathcal{C}^* \leftarrow \mathcal{C}^* \cup \mathcal{R}_2[:5]$
    \IF{$\text{TerminationCondition}(\mathcal{G}, \mathcal{F}, h)$ is TRUE}
        \STATE \textbf{break}
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $\mathcal{C}^*$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Here, $H_{\max}$ denotes the maximum hop depth ($H_{\max} = 2$ in the diagnostic setup). The predicate $\text{TerminationCondition}(\mathcal{G}, \mathcal{F}, h)$ evaluates to \textbf{TRUE} if all sub-goals in the decomposition plan $\mathcal{G}$ are grounded by extracted facts in $\mathcal{F}$ or if hop $h$ yields no new bridging entities ($\mathcal{E}_h = \emptyset$). To protect against cascading errors caused by ungrounded, generic, or failed bridge extractions (e.g., when an extractor outputs ``N/A'' or extraction refusal phrases), the procedure enforces a confidence and grounding gate: if $\text{Confidence}(\mathcal{E}_h) < \tau$ or if extracted tokens fail lexical grounding in first-hop context, the planner falls back to single-pass hybrid search on the original compound query $Q$, designed to mitigate cascading bridge errors and reduce retrieval degradation below single-pass hybrid search when intermediate bridging is uncertain.

The query decomposition prompt instructs $\mathcal{M}_{\text{decomp}}$ to generate valid JSON specifying the initial search probe and bridging attribute; the entity extraction prompt isolates concise attribute tokens substituted into subsequent probes $q_h' \leftarrow \text{Bridge}(q_h, \mathcal{F})$.

As formalized in Algorithm \ref{alg:multihop}, consider an illustrative cross-modal query connecting a visual scene with an acoustic event: (1)~\textbf{Hop 1 (Visual Extraction):} Sub-query $q_1$ retrieves photographic evidence via hybrid matching; entity extraction yields target entity $E_1 = \text{``thunderstorm sky''}$. (2)~\textbf{Entity Bridging:} The bridge operator creates cross-modal probe $q_2' = \text{``severe acoustic weather thunderstorm recording''}$. (3)~\textbf{Hop 2 (Acoustic Retrieval):} Sub-query $q_2'$ queries the acoustic archive using dense embeddings and metadata to retrieve the target recording.

\subsection{RAMP-RAG: Reliability-Aware Adaptive Fusion and Cascade Hypotheses}
\label{sec:ramp_rag}
To systematically investigate candidate architectural mechanisms for handling proxy noise and latency constraints, we formulate the \textbf{RAMP-RAG} hypothesis framework, evaluating three distinct components individually and jointly:
\begin{enumerate}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{1pt plus 0.5pt}
    \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis H1 (Reliability-Aware Clean-Stream Fusion):} Multimedia items maintain multiple proxy streams $m \in \{\text{caption}, \text{ocr}, \text{vector}\}$ weighted by observable reliability $r_m(d) \in [0, 1]$. To avoid oracle leakage of injected corruption levels, $r_{\text{ocr}}(d) = \bar{c}_{\text{ocr}}(d) \cdot \rho_{\text{vocab}}(d)$, where $\bar{c}_{\text{ocr}}$ is mean word recognition confidence and $\rho_{\text{vocab}}$ is the valid dictionary token ratio computed directly from candidate text. Because text noise corrupts both dense and sparse text representations, we introduce an uncorrupted visual clean stream $\hat{S}_{\text{clean}}$ (e.g., zero-shot CLIP ViT-B/32 image embeddings):
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        S_{\text{RAMP}}(q, d) &= \alpha_q \hat{S}_{\text{dense}} + (1 - \alpha_q) r_{\text{ocr}}(d) \hat{S}_{\text{sparse}} \\
        &\quad + \gamma (1 - r_{\text{ocr}}(d)) \hat{S}_{\text{clean}}
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:ramp}
    \end{equation}
    where $\gamma$ is tuned on the Dev split ($\gamma^* = 0.80$) to dynamically allocate mass to the corruption-invariant stream as proxy reliability drops.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis H2 (Query-Adaptive Router $\alpha_q$):} Instead of static weights, a lightweight classifier extracts surface cues $\phi(q) = [r_{\text{cjk}}, r_{\text{digit}}, \mathbb{I}_{\text{upper}}, \mathbb{I}_{\text{id\_cue}}, \text{len}(q), \text{avg\_idf}]$ to dynamically adjust $\alpha_q$, testing whether surface features alone can route queries between sparse and dense streams.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis H3 (Confidence-Gated and Conformal Cascade Ranking):} Stage 1 scores candidate pools and calculates the decision margin between top candidates: $\Delta_{1,2} = s^{(1)} - s^{(2)}$. When candidates are distinct ($\Delta_{1,2} \ge \tau$), Stage 1 ranking is accepted. For ambiguous margins ($\Delta_{1,2} < \tau$), top candidates escalate to a neural cross-encoder (\texttt{ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2} or \texttt{BAAI/bge-reranker-base}). Beyond a fixed margin heuristic ($\tau = 0.235$), we evaluate split conformal calibration targeting expected marginal miss risk $\le \epsilon = 0.05$ with distribution-free risk guarantees.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Integration with Multi-Hop Reasoning:} Under this cascade design, multi-hop decomposition benefits from candidate filtering, escalating ambiguous intermediate steps to neural cross-encoders while accepting distinct first-stage retrievals.


\section{Empirical Regime Analysis of Sparse, Dense, and Native-Visual Retrieval Under Proxy Noise}
\label{sec:regime_analysis}
\label{sec:public_regime_analysis}

To rigorously evaluate the empirical boundaries of text-proxy retrieval without annotation leakage, we benchmarked an expanded public multimodal collection under a rigorous pre-specified experimental protocol across held-out evaluation splits. We structure our evaluation across three canonical operational regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{0.2pt plus 0.1pt}
    \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{Regime A (Text-Dominated):} Scanned receipts and administrative forms evaluated under real optical character recognition (ICDAR 2019 SROIE \cite{huang2019sroie} and FUNSD \cite{jaume2019funsd}), isolating sub-word lexical dominance, micro-mechanisms of dense collapse, and production reranking cascades.
    \item \textbf{Regime B (Visual-Dominated):} Photographic scene search under synthetic caption proxies ($N=1{,}000$ MS-COCO Karpathy test images \cite{lin2014coco}), establishing where native vision-language embeddings decisively surpass machine-generated text representations.
    \item \textbf{Regime C (Cross-Modal and Multi-Hop):} Compositional relational queries spanning modalities, establishing where rule-based extractive pseudo-relevance feedback matches iterative generative LLM bridging without API overhead.
\end{itemize}
Preliminary feasibility pilots over internal personal album photos and synthetic noise sweeps are documented in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_pilot}.

\subsection{Benchmark Scale, Partitions, and Optical Degradation Pipeline}
The public benchmark integrates 626 authentic scanned receipt documents from ICDAR 2019 SROIE \cite{huang2019sroie} and 1,000 photographic distractors from MS-COCO \cite{lin2014coco}, establishing a corpus of 1,626 pooled multimodal items and 612 factual probes. To prevent data snooping, the corpus is partitioned into a strict 50/50 Dev/Test split by Item ID:
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{0.2pt plus 0.1pt}
    \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{Dev Split ($N=306$ probes, 813 corpus items):} Dedicated strictly to hyperparameter tuning (e.g., hybrid fusion coefficient $\alpha^*$, visual weight $\gamma^*$, and conformal risk thresholds $\tau^*$).
    \item \textbf{Held-Out Test Split ($N=306$ probes, 813 corpus items):} Frozen for evaluation across Seeds 42, 43, and 44 with zero test-split tuning.
\end{itemize}

Document text representations are extracted using Apple's Vision framework (\texttt{VNRecognizeTextRequest}) under two distinct conditions: (1)~\emph{Clean OCR}, yielding normalized character error rate (CER) = 0.2848 and word error rate (WER) = 0.3928 under standardized document-level Levenshtein alignment \cite{schneider2024jiwer}; and (2)~\emph{Realistic Optical Degradation}, applying Gaussian blur ($\sigma=1.5$), bilinear 50\% downscaling, random skew ($\pm 2.5^\circ$), and aggressive JPEG re-compression ($Q=20$), elevating CER to 0.3822 and WER to 0.5691. Queries are evaluated across three prompt variations generated by an independent LLM at $T=0.0$ (\texttt{gpt-4o-mini} \cite{openai2024gpt4o}): verbatim lookups (100\% token overlap), natural light paraphrases (41.2\% mean token overlap), and natural answerable questions (preserving key factual anchors while enforcing natural interrogative phrasing).

Thus, semantic paraphrase mismatch dominates mild-to-moderate OCR noise, but severe optical blur induces comparable or greater retrieval collapse. System builders must balance query-side reformulation with robust sub-word indexing.

To isolate why dense vector representations collapse under proxy corruptions while sparse inverted indexes endure, we analyzed three underlying mechanisms (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism_plots}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{0.2pt plus 0.1pt}
    \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{WordPiece Tokenization Fragmentation:} As character corruption increases from 0\% to 40\%, WordPiece tokenization fragments words into multiple subwords. Mean subwords per whitespace-delimited word escalates from 1.347 (clean) to 1.774 (10\% noise), 2.246 (20\% noise), and \textbf{2.994} (40\% noise, a \textbf{+122.3\% fragmentation increase}).
    \item \textbf{Catastrophic Embedding Drift:} This subword fragmentation shifts contextual attention weights, driving acute embedding drift. Relative to uncorrupted text, \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} mean cosine similarity collapses from 1.000 to 0.7301 (10\% noise), \textbf{0.4655} (20\% noise), and \textbf{0.2604} (40\% noise). \texttt{BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5} displays greater structural resistance (cosine similarity 0.7007 at 20\% noise, 0.5595 at 40\%), explaining its superior noise resilience in Table~\ref{tab:regime_grid_v3}.
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic Error Attribution across Probe Properties:} Stratifying test probes into five property buckets under 20\% noise exposes distinct vulnerability profiles. Dense bi-encoders collapse almost completely on \emph{Numeric-Heavy} probes (MiniLM MRR = 0.033, BGE = 0.081) and \emph{Short} entity probes (MiniLM MRR = 0.068, BGE = 0.135). Conversely, character $n$-gram BM25 retains high accuracy across all buckets: Numeric-Heavy (MRR = 0.415), Short entities (0.473), Rare Tokens (0.643), and Named Entities (0.504).
\end{enumerate}

To verify whether this dense degradation is an artifact of sequence length truncation, we evaluated token length distributions across all 626 SROIE receipts. While only 4 receipts (0.64\%) exceed 512 tokens, the real parameter limit for \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} is $\text{max\_seq\_length}=256$, where 209 of 626 receipts (33.39\%) exceed 256 WordPiece tokens. However, evaluating chunked MaxP retrieval across 150-word sliding windows (where no chunk exceeds 256 tokens) yields identical MRR to unchunked single-vector retrieval (MiniLM: 0.2030 chunked vs.\ 0.2056 unchunked; BGE-base: 0.3028 chunked vs.\ 0.3022 unchunked), demonstrating that sequence truncation is not the primary cause of dense collapse.

Crucially, as documented in Table~\ref{tab:dense_formulation_v6}, dense bi-encoder collapse on pristine ground truth text ($0\%$ noise) is heavily governed by query formulation mismatch. Short isolated verbatim snippets trigger severe representation disconnect (MiniLM MRR = 0.1919, BGE-Base MRR = 0.3256). Length-padding or wrapping snippets into full sentential contexts does not alleviate the deficit. However, co-specifying multiple anchor entities (Keyword-Matched Probes) improves BGE-Base MRR to 0.5500 (Hit@10 78.43\%), and formulating queries as natural answerable questions improves BGE-Base MRR to 0.5263 (Hit@10 79.74\%) on the identical uncorrupted document text. Dense bi-encoders are trained on natural language asymmetric question-passage pairs; when queries mirror real-world multi-entity lookups or user questions rather than isolated OCR substrings, dense retrieval recovers substantial discriminative capacity.

Furthermore, to establish the causal impact of tokenization fragmentation versus query formulation, we evaluated a vocabulary projection intervention against the clean Dev lexicon under 20\% character noise. Reconstructing broken word tokens restored 65.3\% of the induced WordPiece subword fragmentation and lifted cosine similarity by $+0.1530$ (from 0.6826 to 0.8356, recovering 48.2\% of embedding drift). In end-to-end retrieval, this intervention improves MiniLM MRR from 0.0570 to 0.1004 (+0.0434 retrieval recovery), while remaining bounded below clean baseline performance (0.2117 MRR). This confirms that query formulation mismatch explains weak dense performance on clean text, while subword tokenization fragmentation explains the additional performance drop under noise.

\subsection{Finite-Sample Conformal Cascades and Risk Guarantees}
\label{sec:conformal_cascades}
\label{sec:conformal_eval}

To establish distribution-free statistical risk guarantees for candidate escalation, we evaluated split conformal calibration across 200 random cluster-stratified 50/50 splits (seeds 1000--1199) on $N=2{,}754$ pooled query instances. Earlier implementations calibrated cascade margins against conditional miss ratios $P(Y \ne 1 \mid \text{not escalated})$, which violate standard monotonic exchangeability guarantees because the conditioning event itself varies with the calibrated threshold $\tau$. When calibrated instead under the standard marginal loss $L = \mathbf{1}[\text{not escalated} \land Y \ne 1]$ with finite-sample adjustment $(n\hat{R}+1)/(n+1) \le \alpha$ \cite{bates2021conformal, angelopoulos2021gentle}, conformal cascade calibration rigorously controls the expected test miss risk at $0.0494 \le 0.05$ at an escalation rate of $78.4\%$ (21.6\% computation savings vs.\ always-escalating cross-encoders; Table~\ref{tab:conformal_cascade_v6}).

Under the exchangeability framework of Bates et al.~\cite{bates2021conformal} and Angelopoulos et al.~\cite{angelopoulos2021gentle}, the theoretical guarantee holds strictly for expected (marginal) risk over calibration-test draws, where the 95\% confidence interval of mean risk $[0.0480, 0.0502]$ covers 0.05. Empirical test miss risk across individual test draws exhibits expected binomial sampling variance: split quantiles are $p_{10} = 0.0355$, $p_{50} = 0.0479$, and $p_{90} = 0.0654$. Stratified by query type, conformal risk is 0.0464 on verbatim lookups and 0.0391 on answerable questions, while entity rewrites experience slightly higher risk (0.0627), refuting any claim of uniform conditional protection across heterogeneous query distributions.

Furthermore, a paired comparison against an equal-escalation fixed-margin baseline calibrated at the identical 78.4\% escalation rate ($\tau = 0.235$) reveals indistinguishable retrieval performance: conformal cascade realized MRR is 0.3577 (verbatim: 0.6932, entity rewrites: 0.3395, answerable questions: 0.0404) versus fixed-margin MRR of 0.3579 (paired difference $-0.0002$, $p > 0.05$). The primary value of conformal prediction lies not in superior ranking accuracy, but in its distribution-free finite-sample guarantee on expected risk.

\subsection{Cross-Engine OCR Replication and Error Characterization}
\label{sec:cross_engine_replication}

To verify that the empirical retrieval hierarchy does not depend on proprietary Apple Vision preprocessing, we replicated the retrieval benchmarks using open-source Tesseract~5.5 LSTM OCR across 626 SROIE receipts (Table~\ref{tab:engine_replication_v3}). Evaluating full-document Levenshtein edit distance via standardized JiWER \cite{schneider2024jiwer} reveals the true transcription quality: clean Tesseract achieves CER = 0.2939 and sequence WER = 0.4879, explaining why character 3--5gram BM25 retains high retrieval accuracy (MRR = 0.8412). Under realistic optical blur, Tesseract error escalates to CER = 0.6377 and WER = 0.8063.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:ocr_error_rates_v6}, we evaluate both sequence Levenshtein WER ($\text{WER}_{\text{seq}}$) and a multiset error rate ($\text{MER}_{\text{BoW}}$); note that the latter is not a standard sequence WER, but evaluates bag-of-words multiset symmetric difference: $\text{MER}_{\text{BoW}} = \frac{\sum_w |c_{\text{ref}}(w) - c_{\text{hyp}}(w)|}{\sum_w c_{\text{ref}}(w)}$. While sequence $\text{WER}_{\text{seq}} = \frac{S + D + I}{N_{\text{ref}}}$ aligns token order and counts an OCR character error as a single token substitution ($S=1$), multiset evaluation penalizes that same error as both a reference omission ($D=1$) and an inserted out-of-vocabulary artifact ($I=1$). When severe OCR corruption splits or mangles words into out-of-vocabulary fragments, multiset symmetric differences escalate sharply (e.g., reaching 0.5919 clean and 1.0043 degraded blur on Tesseract), although on clean Apple Vision OCR where word boundaries remain largely intact, multiset error is lower than sequence Levenshtein WER (0.2476 vs.\ 0.3928).

Quantifying retrieval performance against human-transcribed ground truth exposes the exact degradation gradient imposed by proxy noise: on verbatim lookups, clean Apple Vision OCR drops 0.0411 MRR vs.\ ground-truth text (0.9443 vs.\ 0.9854 for Char BM25), clean Tesseract drops 0.1442 MRR (0.8412 vs.\ 0.9854), and degraded optical blur on Tesseract drops 0.5845 MRR (0.4009 vs.\ 0.9854). Across both engines, character 3--5gram BM25 retains top MRR under verbatim lookups (0.9443 Apple, 0.8412 Tesseract), while dense bi-encoders suffer severe degradation (MiniLM MRR drops to 0.1650 clean and 0.0936 degraded on Tesseract).

\subsection{Practical Implications and Production Deployment Guidelines}
\label{sec:practical_implications}
To translate our empirical findings into actionable engineering recommendations for system architects and retrieval engineers, Table~\ref{tab:decision_guide_v6} synthesizes four core deployment rules:
\begin{enumerate}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{1pt plus 0.5pt}
    \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{For Document Search System Architects:} For administrative receipts, invoices, and standard scanned forms, character 3--5gram BM25 should serve as the default primary retrieval engine. It provides high precision (MRR $> 0.84$) across commercial and open-source OCR engines at sub-millisecond CPU latency ($0.87$\,ms), outperforming neural bi-encoders without requiring GPU infrastructure. Neural cross-encoder reranking should be triggered selectively only when user queries exhibit low lexical overlap with document metadata.
    \item \textbf{For Photographic and Image-Centric Archives:} Native vision-language embeddings (e.g., SigLIP or CLIP) are mandatory for searching natural photographs. Machine-generated caption proxies lose approximately 40\% of retrieval accuracy (MRR 0.3728 vs.\ 0.6071, $p < 10^{-4}$) due to semantic caption bottlenecks, and text-only search should not be relied upon for photographic discovery.
    \item \textbf{For Multi-Hop Relational Reasoning:} Production systems should deploy rule-based extractive pseudo-relevance feedback as the primary bridging mechanism. Extractive bridging matches iterative generative LLM bridging in retrieval accuracy (Target Hit@10 = 0.820) while completely eliminating LLM token latency and external API costs.
    \item \textbf{For Computational and Energy Budgeting:} Document-native multi-vector models (ColQwen2) deliver valuable semantic lift under paraphrased queries (+0.0982 MRR), but impose $286\times$ higher latency (249.21\,ms vs.\ 0.87\,ms) and require $\sim$6\,GB of dedicated GPU VRAM. Conformal cascade ranking enables systems to capture neural accuracy gains while saving over 21\% of compute overhead with mathematically bounded risk.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
To facilitate objective peer assessment, we document the critical limitations of this study:
\begin{enumerate}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{0.2pt plus 0.1pt}
    \setlength{\parsep}{0pt}
    \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{Benchmark domain concentration:} Our primary text retrieval evaluations focus predominantly on a single document-type family (retail receipts from SROIE, $N=626$) alongside scanned administrative forms from FUNSD ($N=100$) and natural images from MS-COCO ($N=1{,}000$). While receipt and form structures capture common real-world OCR artifacts, document layouts with multi-column prose, rich typography, or complex financial tables may exhibit differing degradation dynamics.
    \item \textbf{Cross-engine OCR generalization:} While Apple Vision represents an advanced commercial neural OCR system, replication across open-source Tesseract 5.5 LSTM confirms that our architectural findings (character $n$-gram robustness and dense collapse) replicate across distinct OCR model architectures and error distributions.
    \item \textbf{Conformal cascade calibration bounds:} Under marginal loss $L = \mathbf{1}[\text{not escalated} \land Y \ne 1]$, split conformal calibration successfully bounds mean test miss risk to $0.0494 \le 0.05$ at an escalation rate of 78.4\% (yielding 21.6\% computation savings vs.\ full cross-encoding). Split coverage across 200 calibration-test draws reaches 58.5\% due to finite-sample sampling variability, reflecting that while expected marginal risk is rigorously controlled, individual finite test draws experience expected binomial variance.
    \item \textbf{Answerable question formulation:} Our answerable questions evaluate natural factual queries isolating semantic abstraction from lexical absence. However, real-world user queries may exhibit broader syntactic and conversational diversity.
    \item \textbf{LLM judge bias and audit sample size:} Automated diagnostic scoring uses \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} \cite{openai2024gpt4o}, which carries documented biases \cite{zheng2023judging}. Mitigated by dual-reviewer human auditing rated by the two co-authors (A. K. Vennela and G. S. B. Koushik) across 110 cases ($\kappa = 0.8716$ faithfulness, $0.8926$ relevance, $0.9451$ correctness, mean $\kappa = 0.9031$). While inter-annotator agreement is high, expanding audits to external non-author panels remains desirable.
    \item \textbf{Multi-hop retrieval utility and latency overhead:} Query decomposition incurs a 45\% latency penalty (4,736.8\,ms vs. 3,260.0\,ms). Paired discordant analysis across 50 multi-hop queries indicates that proposed multi-hop LLM bridging provides no statistically detectable retrieval improvement over single-stage hybrid retrieval (+1 net hit / 50; exact McNemar $p = 1.000$) or single-stage dense retrieval (identical 32/50 hits).
    \item \textbf{Dose-response non-linearity:} The retrieval drop under query mismatch is non-monotonic across query types: light paraphrases with entity substitutions trigger severe drops ($\Delta = 0.3782$ MRR), whereas answerable factual questions preserve key topical anchors and sustain high lexical retrieval ($\text{MRR} = 0.9471$).
    \item \textbf{Synthetic vs.\ optical noise divergence:} Character $n$-gram robustness and dictionary-based reliability weighting tuned on synthetic character permutations fail to translate directly to real optical OCR blur. System architects must validate on physical optical degradation rather than synthetic character noise.
    \item \textbf{LLM-generated query synthesis and single generator threat:} Evaluation queries for light paraphrases and answerable questions were synthesized using \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} \cite{openai2024gpt4o}. Although audited across a 50-probe sample against a second generator (Qwen2.5-72B), model-generated queries may under-represent human typographical errors, spontaneous colloquialisms, or ambiguous intent.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
In this work, we have established that intermediate textual proxies in multimodal retrieval-augmented generation exhibit well-defined, regime-dependent efficacy boundaries rather than uniform success or failure. Our empirical investigation across 1,626 multimodal items reveals three principal architectural insights.

First, lexical inverted indexing over character sub-words ($n$-grams of length 3--5) provides an extraordinarily resilient sparse retrieval foundation, outperforming standard word tokenization across both commercial neural OCR and open-source engines under heavy optical blur; whole-word and dense encoders only surpass sub-word sparse matching when search queries diverge into semantic paraphrases. Second, the frequently observed collapse of dense bi-encoders on administrative text reflects a query-span formulation mismatch rather than sequence truncation; structuring user queries as contextual answerable questions restores semantic discriminability without modifying document vectors. Third, native visual representations are indispensable for unconstrained photographic imagery, where automated caption proxies suffer severe information bottlenecks; however, on structured document pages, document-native vision-language models yield statistically indistinguishable accuracy from CPU lexical search on factual questions while imposing two orders of magnitude higher latency.

These findings directly challenge the emerging assumption that end-to-end multi-vector vision models must universally replace OCR-mediated indexing. Instead, we show that lightweight sub-word lexical inverted indexes on CPU remain optimal for high-throughput verbatim and entity-centric search, while cross-encoder cascades should be reserved selectively for paraphrased queries via conformal risk control. Furthermore, rule-based extractive entity bridging matches multi-hop generative LLM techniques without incurring recurring token latency or cloud API costs.

Future research will extend this regime framework along three axes: (1) expanding held-out evaluation to multilingual and handwritten administrative archives; (2) designing adaptive conformal thresholding conditioned on real-time query difficulty estimators; and (3) investigating sparse-guided selective caching for vision-language models. By formalizing the trade-offs between computational overhead and representation fidelity, this work provides a rigorous engineering roadmap for efficient, trustworthy multimodal retrieval in real-world computing systems.

\section{Reviewer-Proofing and Out-of-Distribution Robustness}
\label{sec:appendix_reviewer_proofing}

\subsection{Verbatim vs. Paraphrased Probes Across OCR Engines}
To verify whether the lexical dominance of character $n$-gram BM25 holds when queries diverge from exact document strings, we evaluated three prompt formulations across both proprietary Apple Vision Neural OCR and open-source Tesseract 5.5 LSTM OCR on the 626 SROIE receipts (Table~\ref{tab:engine_replication_v3}). Under verbatim lookups, character 3--5gram BM25 captures broken substrings, vendor abbreviations, and numeric totals with high precision (0.9443 Apple, 0.8412 Tesseract). However, when queries undergo entity-substituting light paraphrasing, word BM25 outperforms character BM25 (0.4003 vs.\ 0.2365 on Apple; 0.3964 vs.\ 0.2265 on Tesseract), while tuned hybrid retrieval maintains balanced accuracy (0.4003 Apple, 0.4023 Tesseract). Under answerable factual questions (heavy paraphrase), topical anchors are restored, and character BM25 regains superiority (0.9558 Apple, 0.8522 Tesseract).

\subsection{Out-of-Distribution Layout Evaluation on Scanned Forms (FUNSD)}
To evaluate whether receipt-derived architectural findings transfer to complex non-receipt administrative forms, Table~\ref{tab:funsd_v3} reports replication across 100 scanned forms from the FUNSD dataset \cite{jaume2019funsd}. On multi-column layouts containing checkboxes, form headers, and tabular field entries:
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{0.2pt plus 0.1pt}
    \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{Narrowing Verbatim Gap:} Character 3--5gram BM25 achieves MRR = 0.8433 on Apple Vision and 0.8066 on Tesseract 5.5, outperforming word BM25 (0.8364 Apple, 0.7763 Tesseract). The verbatim margin narrows from $+0.1065$ (receipts) to $+0.0069$ (forms), reflecting longer form sentences where whole-word inverted indexing matches multi-word headers directly.
    \item \textbf{Paraphrase Hybrid Recovery:} Under light paraphrasing, word BM25 drops to 0.1573, while character BM25 achieves 0.2228 and tuned hybrid reaches 0.2099. Dense BGE-base provides 0.1834, preventing complete lexical omission.
\end{itemize}
